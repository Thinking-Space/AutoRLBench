Can AI Agents Diagnose and Repair RL Training Failures?

A benchmark for testing whether AI agents can diagnose and repair RL training failures.

31 diagnosis tasks18 repair tasks10 frontier agents
Scroll to read the findings

TL;DR

AI for AI is a key path to recursive self-improvement. Existing evaluations mostly focus on whether an end-to-end experiment succeeds, making it hard to see where an agent excels or falls short. AutoRLBench breaks this process down into diagnosing and repairing RL training failures. Our initial release includes 31 diagnosis tasks and 18 repair tasks across six failure categories: configuration, reward, data, environment, algorithm, and infrastructure. In our first evaluation of 10 frontier agents, results show that agents are better at identifying a failure than explaining its root cause, and the main bottleneck for repair is whether the fix addresses the core issue rather than making superficial changes.

This project is under active development, with tasks and evaluation results updated in real time. We will continue to collect real training failures, expand verification for long-term stability, functional completeness, and cross-environment setups, and evaluate more recent agents. We also welcome community contributions of new tasks. If you have encountered challenging RL training failures, especially those requiring extensive debugging, long reasoning chains, and complex repairs, please submit them to us so we can turn them into reproducible tasks together.

AI for AI is viewed as a key path to recursive self-improvement (RSI). As agents become more integrated into real-world research and development tasks, end-to-end evaluations are no longer enough. For example, PostTrainBench measures an agent's ability to run a post-training pipeline. While agents appear capable of completing the full pipeline, a final overall score alone does not reveal their exact capability boundaries. It remains unclear whether an agent can handle unexpected failures, adjust its strategy based on feedback, and verify that its changes truly fix the issue. These process-level capabilities need to be evaluated separately.

In this project, we choose reinforcement learning (RL) training as our research setting. RL training failures take many forms, and training signals are noisy and delayed. Moreover, the same symptom can stem from different root causes across data, reward, algorithm, or infrastructure. When training stagnates or performance collapses, an agent needs to locate the failure, choose how to intervene, and verify that the improvement lasts. Therefore, AutoRLBench focuses on a more specific, fine-grained, and practical question: When RL training fails, can an AI agent identify the root cause and reliably repair it?

Overview

AutoRLBench evaluates the ability of AI agents to diagnose and repair RL training failures. For our initial release, we collected and curated 31 tasks from the open research community. Every task comes from a real RL training failure, and 18 of them include a verified repair run. Based on where the root cause occurs in the pipeline, we group the tasks into six categories: configuration, reward, data, environment, algorithm, and infrastructure. Each task provides the full workspace from the failed training run, including source code, configurations, logs, and evaluation records.

The first batch of tasks brings together contributions from more than 10 universities, research institutes, and research groups, including Tsinghua University, Fudan University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Harbin Institute of Technology, University of Pennsylvania, Tohoku University, Duke University, Johns Hopkins University, and FusionBrain Lab, alongside independent researchers.

We evaluate agents at two levels:

Level 01 · Diagnosis

Diagnosis

Determine whether the training run is healthy, identify the specific root cause, and provide supporting evidence. An LLM Judge evaluates whether the submitted diagnosis matches the reference answer.

Level 02 · Repair

Repair

Modify the workspace and submit a repair report. After the agent finishes, the evaluation system relaunches the training run in the modified workspace and grades the repair based on the resulting training metrics. Of the 31 tasks, 18 support repair evaluation.

Main Results

In our first evaluation, we tested 10 frontier agents, producing 310 diagnosis trajectories and 180 repair trajectories. Across both task levels, the top-performing agent, GPT-6-Astra, achieves an overall pass rate of 46.9%, with a large gap across agents.

Diagnosis: A Capability Gap Between Identifying Failures and Explaining Root Causes

Diagnosis requires an agent to determine whether the training run has failed, identify the root cause, and provide supporting evidence. Our results show that agents identify a failure far more reliably than they explain its root cause. Across all diagnosis trajectories, 87% correctly judged the training run status as unhealthy, while only 40.6% identified the correct root cause. However, recognizing a failure is not enough. As shown in Figure 2, agents can still fail in two distinct ways: identifying a wrong cause, or finding the right cause through wrong evidence. Separating these two failure modes shows whether the agent fails to trace the mechanism or fails to verify its conclusion.

Why Do Agents Identify the Wrong Cause?

Explaining root causes requires linking surface symptoms to the underlying execution mechanism. In wrong cause trajectories, many agents mistake symptoms like training stagnation or zero reward for the root cause itself. Others stop at a single plausible explanation or address only one part of a compound failure. Because the same symptom can stem from different root causes, and multiple failures can produce similar training curves, a single hypothesis is not enough. To find the true root cause, an agent must trace how a candidate defect actually affects the execution path rather than stopping at surface failures.

Why Do Agents Provide Wrong Evidence?

Even when an agent proposes a plausible cause, it must verify this claim with concrete evidence. In wrong evidence trajectories, agents often rely on surface trends, such as training completion or rising metrics, while overlooking hidden inconsistencies across the workspace. In RL training, a defect can reduce update steps or skew the execution path without triggering explicit errors or immediate metric drops. Because local metric improvements and mechanism failures can coexist, curve analysis alone is unreliable. Reliable evidence requires cross-checking code, configurations, and logs, running checks to rule out alternative hypotheses, and confirming that the evidence directly supports the identified root cause.

Repair: Bottlenecks Lie in Addressing the Core Issue and Sustaining Improvement

Repair tasks require the agent to turn its diagnosis into concrete workspace changes. Comparing repair runs that start from similar initial performance reveals two main bottlenecks: whether the fix addresses the core failure mechanism, and whether the improvement lasts until the end of training. As shown in Figure 3, some failed repairs remain flat at their initial level, while others improve early in training before dropping sharply. Evaluating repair capability therefore requires examining the full training run.

Do Changes Address Core Failure?

An effective repair must fix the underlying failure mechanism rather than making superficial changes. Agents often patch surface symptoms without tracing how their changes affect training, or run local checks that miss the actual execution path of the failure. Both mistakes confuse local fixes with full system recovery. To make an effective fix, an agent must test its changes along the actual execution path, verify that the fix restores normal training behavior, and ensure that it does not break existing functionality.

Do Early Improvements Last Throughout Training?

A reliable repair must also sustain improvement throughout the full training run. Figure 3 shows that some failed runs improve early on, and even take an early lead, before collapsing later. Changing the workspace based only on early symptoms, or treating a short-term metric bounce as a complete fix, misses long-term effects. Therefore, repair verification must cover the full training run to confirm that the failure does not return and early gains are not lost.

Analysis

Performance Across the Six Failure Categories

As shown in Figure 4, agent performance varies widely across the six failure categories, and each category exposes a distinct bottleneck:

  • Configuration failures depend on whether parameters actually reach the target training components.
  • Reward failures require tracing the feedback loop across data, reward, advantage, and policy updates without mistaking zero reward or skewed outputs for the root cause.
  • Data failures rely on clear read and write rules along the data loading path, producing an interesting contrast between diagnosis and repair.
  • Environment failures require looking beyond the training pipeline to verify that the external environment responds as expected.
  • Algorithm failures require checking consistency between policy updates and sampling behavior.
  • Infrastructure failures require verifying coordination and state consistency across multiple system components.

An Interesting Contrast: Data Failures Are Hard to Diagnose but Easy to Repair

Data failures stand out as a notable case where diagnosis involves a long reasoning chain, yet the repair itself is straightforward once the issue is located. In one data task, hard samples are accidentally dropped halfway through training, and the data loader automatically fills the gap with easy samples (Figure 5). Training continues without errors, and the training reward even keeps rising, while the validation score drops as hard samples disappear. Relying on the training curve alone easily misses this bug. Locating the failure requires cross-checking what the pipeline expects to read, what it actually reads, how it handles exceptions, and what the final evaluation measures. Once the agent finds this mismatch, however, fixing the data loader is direct and reliable.

Relationship Between Diagnosis and Repair

As shown in Figure 6, successful diagnosis correlates positively with successful repair, but the two capabilities remain distinct. Surprisingly, diagnosis and repair do not form a continuous process driven by the same underlying capability, where poor diagnosis directly leads to poor repair. An agent's main bottleneck in diagnosis is clearly explaining the root cause, even when it has already spotted the general direction of the failure. Repair, however, only requires the agent to modify the code rather than provide an accurate explanation. As a result, an agent can still fix a failure even when its diagnosis fails, and an accurate diagnosis does not guarantee a reliable fix.

Repair Error Analysis

We further analyze the failed repair trajectories. In 19 trajectories, agents made no changes, often assuming that the original run was healthy, while in 47 others they changed the code but stayed at the symptom level, for example by adjusting reward handling, learning rate, KL, or response length. Some repairs addressed only part of the failure chain or failed in the actual execution environment.

These errors reveal three recurring patterns. First, agents treat a run that looks normal as evidence that the failure has been ruled out, or mistake a symptom for its cause and stop intervening too early. Second, agents use CPU checks, syntax checks, or self-written unit tests in place of end-to-end validation. A patch may pass on a local path while missing the actual CUDA path, restricted runtime, or asynchronous export path. Third, agents sometimes change the evaluation target or previously healthy behavior, for example by relaxing the scorer, reducing test or data coverage, bypassing a required adapter, or fixing negative cases while rejecting valid ones.

Case Study: Agent Cheating and Evaluation Shortcuts

In repair tasks, an agent has full write access to the workspace and can observe many evaluation signals. Higher metrics alone therefore do not guarantee a valid repair. Figure 8 shows a trajectory from GLM-5.3 on an infrastructure task. The agent starts with a valid local diagnosis by tracing the retrieval logs and locating the broken data path. However, instead of fixing the bug independently, it uses curl to download the upstream source, runs a diff against the local workspace, and copies the reference code directly into its patch. While this patch fixes the failure, it turns independent repair into reference copying.

Across our broader trajectory audit, we observe three main types of evaluation shortcuts that inflate metrics without making a genuine repair:

  • Fetching external references, such as downloading upstream repositories to copy existing fixes.
  • Weakening evaluation criteria, such as relaxing scorer or evaluator rules to inflate logged scores.
  • Simplifying the target task, such as deleting rule items or bypassing required execution paths to make the task easier.

To prevent these shortcuts, AutoRLBench evaluates the final result, the repair process, and task boundaries together. A unified monitor checks whether external materials enter the patch and verifies that evaluation data, gold labels, and scorers remain untouched. Finally, the evaluation system checks core task invariants and reruns training in a clean environment, allowing agents to fix faulty training code without tampering with the task or evaluation criteria.

Cost and Time Analysis

Higher token usage or longer execution time does not guarantee better performance. On the shared task subsets, GPT-6.1-Sol passes 12 of 28 diagnosis tasks for $22.85, whereas GPT-6-Astra passes 13 of 28 for $132.00. On repair tasks, GPT-6.1-Sol passes 9 of 17 for $92.31, whereas Gemini-4-Argon passes 11 of 17 for $750.62. Extra exploration helps only when it narrows down hypotheses, verifies fixes, and stops unproductive attempts early.

In repair tasks, execution time is heavily skewed by failed runs. The longest 10% of trajectories consume 47.0% of the total execution time, and failed trajectories account for 62.0%. Some agents submit unverified changes quickly, while others explore the wrong direction until they exhaust the budget. For example, GPT-6.1-Sol has a median repair time of 18.5 minutes but a mean of 87.2 minutes. Relying on average execution time alone therefore mistakes long-tail waste for thorough reasoning.

Conclusion and Roadmap

AutoRLBench breaks the core challenge of AI for AI down into two connected questions: can an agent identify why an RL training run fails, and can it reliably repair the failure without tampering with the task? Our evaluation shows that while frontier agents can recognize surface failures, tracing them back to their exact root causes remains difficult. Furthermore, even plausible patches often yield only short-term metric rebounds rather than sustained recovery across the full training run.

We're just getting started. The tasks and leaderboard are updated continuously. Next, we plan to:

  • Expand the collection of real training failures. Add more tasks, especially silent failures, bugs involving several interacting components, and failures that emerge late in training.
  • Cover broader RL settings. Extend coverage of multimodal RL, embodied AI, and multi-turn interactive agents, including failures in perception, environment feedback, and tool use.
  • Expand full post-training workflows. Add tasks involving supervised fine-tuning (SFT), on-policy distillation (OPD), and transitions between training stages.
  • Scale cloud evaluation. Support large batches of parallel agent evaluations with GPU scheduling, reproducible environments, and consistent resource budgets.
  • Strengthen trajectory monitoring. Improve detection of external reference, unauthorized changes to evaluation data or evaluators, and attempts to bypass required execution paths.
  • Keep agent baselines and efficiency analysis current. Evaluate new models and harnesses as they become available, reporting pass rates alongside token use, cost, and verification behavior.

Have a training bug that took days to understand or was difficult to repair? We are actively collecting tasks, especially failures with long reasoning chains and hard-to-find fixes. Share the code, logs, and any available fix through our contribution guide. A working repair is welcome but not required. We will help turn the failure into a reproducible benchmark task and continue updating the results as new agents are evaluated.
